apostrophe

Verified

A straight ' in a word or before a year becomes ’

Why: A typographic apostrophe is ’, not the straight typewriter mark, and the same ’ opens an elided word (’90s, rock ’n’ roll) instead of an opening quote.

Others, gaps, unread: Matches the engine, including the leading-apostrophe cases fixed in the CHANGELOG. Butterick makes an exception for the Hawaiian okina (‘Oʻahu’), which the engine does not know. English only; no source for other languages was read.

ellipsis

Verified

... becomes …

Why: Three dots are one character, …, so they cannot break across lines.

Others, gaps, unread: Butterick agrees and also asks for a no-break space on a side with no text so the ellipsis stays with its text; the engine does not do that (`... and so` keeps an ordinary space, a gap in the backlog). dash.dialogue

Verified

A paragraph opening with a dash is a line of dialogue, in languages that write dialogue that way: their dash and a no-break space (Ukrainian — Привіт)

Why: In these languages a line of dialogue opens with a dash, not a quotation mark, so a paragraph that opens with a typed hyphen and a space is such a line. The dash is the one the language uses for it, followed by a no-break space (attached with no space in Spanish), in both registers.

Others, gaps, unread: Which dash: the sources set the em dash for uk, es and pt; pl uses whatever dash the text uses (the pack uses the em dash); fr accepts both (em set); bg says long dash but renders the en dash and it shows the en dash, and for both the pack keeps the em dash of its ISG sentence dash so that dialogue and the narrator match; ro, hr, hu, sk, lt, cs, sl, fi and sv set the en dash; da says the same form as the tankestreg, the em dash in the pack. Only the OQLF asks for a no-break space after the dash; the engine sets one everywhere but Spanish so that the dash never ends a line alone. Not set: et (quotes are the norm, EKI), lv and el (the dash is not confirmed by a source read), and en, de, ch, nl, mt, ga (dialogue is quoted). A paragraph whose later line also opens with a dash is read as a list and left alone. The 2019 Ukrainian Pravopys (§ 161) could not be read; § 125 of the earlier edition was. In cs, sl and da a dash is one of several allowed forms.

number.group

Unchecked

Digits of an integer of five or more are grouped in threes with the language separator (1,000,000; 1 000 000)

Why: Integers of five or more digits are grouped in threes with the language separator (comma, no-break space, dot) so a long number can be read.

Others, gaps, unread: Opt-in rule (off unless named in `enable`). The SI brochure (9th ed., 5.4.4) says digits "may be divided into groups of three by a space", that "neither dots nor commas are ever inserted in the spaces between groups", that with only four digits before or after the decimal marker a space is not used to isolate a single digit, and that the practice "is a matter of choice". The engine agrees on groups of three, on starting at five digits, and on the space itself for the languages whose pack uses a no-break space (the brochure does not say nonbreaking). It deliberately differs where the pack groups with a comma (en) or a dot, which the brochure forbids; that is local custom, not SI. The brochure is a metrology standard and does not speak for ordinary prose in any one language, so the choice stays general-knowledge. Separators per language come from the packs and are otherwise unverified; Swiss usage differs (the pack follows the Federal Chancellery, not read here).

dash.sentence

Verified

A spaced hyphen, dash or double hyphen becomes a spaced dash (em; en in German and literary British English), no-break before it

Why: A break in a sentence takes a dash, and its form depends on the language. For English the em dash is backed by the source; it is the engine default.

Others, gaps, unread: The source sets the em dash flush against the words and calls spaces acceptable; the engine sets it spaced, with a no-break space before it, which matches neither. A search result quoting Bringhurst (The Elements of Typographic Style, 2nd ed., section 5.2.1, not read here) says he prefers a spaced en dash in English, so the primary guides disagree among themselves. UAX #14 gives the em dash class B2, which allows a break on both sides, hence the no-break space before it. The spaced en dash for German and for British literary English is not backed by any source read yet. For ro, hr, hu, sk and sv the spaced en dash comes from the dialogue sources: each sets the same spaced en dash before the words of the narrator, and the packs had no verified sentence dash before.

symbol.minus

Unchecked

A hyphen before a digit (-5) becomes a minus sign (−5)

Why: A minus sign is −, not a hyphen.

Others, gaps, unread: The SI brochure, 9th ed., has no passage on the minus sign: its section 5.4.4 only shows the leading zero (-0.234 but not -.234) and says nothing of the character to use, so it does not back this rule. Unicode only shows that the character exists; it does not say when to use it. space.percent

Verified

German, French, Czech, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Irish, Maltese, Bulgarian, Croatian, Slovenian, Lithuanian, Latvian, Estonian, Danish, Finnish and Greek: a no-break space between a number and % (5 %); German, French, Czech, Spanish, Italian, Bulgarian, Croatian, Slovenian, Danish, Finnish and Greek: also before € after the number (49 €)

Why: German and French set a no-break space before % and a currency sign. CLDR agrees for % in de and fr, and for the currency sign in de and fr; it sets no space before % in en, uk, pl, de-CH and fr-CH, which is what the engine does. OQLF, a printed-style authority, agrees for French: a no-break space before %, $ and €. For € alone, the Greek, Finnish, Italian and Danish editions of the Interinstitutional Style Guide (part 3, 7) write the symbol after the figure with a space (30 €), as does the Czech handbook (250 €, with a fixed space), so currencySpace stays on in el, fi, it, da and cs.

Others, gaps, unread: The space before % and the space before € are two pack fields, percentSpace and currencySpace: Irish, Maltese, Portuguese, Latvian, Lithuanian and Estonian set the first and not the second, because their ISG editions write €30 with no space or show money only as the code EUR (see each pack's notes). CLDR also puts a no-break space before the currency sign in uk, pl and fr-CH; the engine leaves 49€ alone there (a gap, in the backlog). OQLF also asks for a no-break space before a French colon, and none or a fine space before ; ! and ?, which the engine does not set (a gap, in the backlog). OQLF is a Quebec source; a France or Swiss guide may differ (a fine space). The German side rests on CLDR only; no Duden page was read. The ISG passage is for the euro only (other currency signs are never spaced by the engine) and, apart from the Finnish edition, does not say the space is nonbreaking; the Czech handbook was read as a fetch-tool summary. The ISG percent rule (6.4) cited in the packs was not re-read here. Of the country guides named in the packs (Kielitoimiston ohjepankki, Retskrivningsordbogen, Treccani), none was read for €.

symbol.degree

Verified

20 C, 20°C and 20 °C become 20 °C, with a no-break space before the unit

Why: A temperature or angle unit gets a no-break space before it (20 °C), and the degree sign is added where the unit letter stands alone.

Others, gaps, unread: Opt-in rule (off unless named in `enable`). The SI brochure, 9th ed., 5.4.3: a space always separates the unit from the number, so "t = 30.2 °C but not t = 30.2°C"; the one exception is the plane-angle degree, minute and second, which take no space (30° 22′ 8″). The engine agrees: it puts a space before ° only when a C or F follows, and leaves a bare angle degree (30°) alone. The brochure does not say the space is nonbreaking (our choice), and it covers the Celsius scale only: the Fahrenheit case (°F) is not SI, so no source was read for it. The label guard (Type 2 F is not a temperature) and the English-only rule for a bare C or F are the engine's own.

space.operator

Unchecked

No-break spaces around & + = standing alone between two words

Why: A lone &, + or = between two words stays on the line of the word before it and of the word after it, so a line neither ends on the operator nor starts with it (Tom & Jerry, rock + roll).

Others, gaps, unread: No source found. Practical Typography "Nonbreaking spaces" was read (as a fetch-tool summary) and does not mention an ampersand or any operator; a web search for a guide on binding an ampersand between names found only general pages on no-break spaces and a LaTeX tie guide for forenames, none a style guide on this point, so none is cited. The rule is a judgement: a line ending on "&" or starting with it reads as a break in the pair. It binds both sides, so "Tom & Jerry" is one unbreakable unit, and a chain of operators binds end to end ("Tom & Jerry & Co" is five tokens in one unit; "one + two + three + four + five" is one run, and a pathological chain could bind a whole line); a guide may prefer a break before the operator instead. Two shapes stay unbound: an operator next to an inline tag in HTML or Markdown ("Tom &amp; <em>Jerry</em>": the tag placeholder is not a letter) and a word ending in a combining mark (decomposed "café & bar"). Numbers (2 + 2, x = 5) are left to the author; doubled operators (&&, ==, +=) and operators with no typed spaces (R&D, x+y) are untouched.

Sources: none read

space.orphan

Verified

No-break space after a one-letter word the language must not strand at a line end

Why: A one-letter word does not end a line.

Others, gaps, unread: Czech is settled by the Internetová jazyková příručka (entry 880, read through a fetch summary): k, s, v, z, o, u, a, i stay with the next word, which is exactly the cs list; the entry says nothing of two-letter prepositions (do, na, ve, ...), so none is bound. Polish (a, i, o, u, w, z): only a Wikipedia article ("Sierotka (typografia)", which cites PWN and PN-P-55366:1983 without our having read either) states the six words, so the list is unchanged and not verified; the PWN Poradnia pages could not be fetched. Ukrainian: no readable source (the one found, a university layout handbook, returned 403 and a search snippet is not a read), so the list is unchanged and not verified. The English a/I rule has no source at all. Neither the Polish nor the Ukrainian two-letter prepositions are bound.

uax14

standard

Unicode Standard Annex #14, Unicode Line Breaking Algorithm, revision 57, Unicode 18.0.0, 2026-09-01 (first 100,000 of 141,504 characters read; line-break class tables and rules LB12, LB12a not read in full).

Relied on: U+00A0 NO-BREAK SPACE is class GL and prohibits a break on either side, and is the preferred way to keep two words on a line (a title and a name); U+202F is the same but narrow; the horizontal ellipsis is class IN, so breaks between consecutive ellipses are never allowed.
