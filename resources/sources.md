# Sources

Why each rule exists: the sources it rests on, the reason it was chosen, and what others do instead. This file is generated by `bun run docs:sources` from `resources/sources.ts`. Do not edit it by hand: `resources/sources.test.ts` fails when it is out of date.

Every source was read before it was entered, on the date given. The `confidence` of a decision is one of:

- `verified`: at least one source read says what the rule does.
- `general-knowledge`: no source read yet; the rule is what the author knows to be usual. Treat it as unchecked.
- `disputed`: a source read says something else than the engine does. See [backlog.md](../docs/backlog.md).

## Decisions

### `quote`

Confidence: `disputed`.

Why: Each language has its own quotation marks, and nesting alternates a second pair. uk (« » „ “) and pl („ ” « ») match CLDR; fr level one and ch (« » ‹ ›, as fr-CH) match too. Butterick agrees that quotes are curly and that styles vary by language, and gives no per-language sets. OQLF puts a no-break space inside French guillemets, as the engine does.

Alternative: CLDR gives English “ ” ‘ ’ (the engine default is « » „ “, a gap already in the backlog as P0), German „ “ ‚ ‘ (the engine sets » « › ‹ on display), French « » with « » inside (the engine uses “ ” inside), and de-CH „ “ ‚ ‘ (the engine ch profile is the fr-CH one). The backlog records that every other tool in the benchmark produces “ ” for English by default (apps/bench/RESULTS.md). Not read: the Swiss Federal Chancellery Schreibweisungen (a PDF this work could not extract text from), Pravopys 2019 (server refused the fetch), Imprimerie nationale Lexique.

### `apostrophe`

Confidence: `verified`.

Why: A typographic apostrophe is ’, not the straight typewriter mark, and the same ’ opens an elided word (’90s, rock ’n’ roll) instead of an opening quote.

Alternative: Matches the engine, including the leading-apostrophe cases fixed in the CHANGELOG. Butterick makes an exception for the Hawaiian okina (‘Oʻahu’), which the engine does not know. English only; no source for other languages was read.

Sources: [butterick-apostrophes](#src-butterick-apostrophes), [butterick-quotes](#src-butterick-quotes)

### `ellipsis`

Confidence: `verified`.

Why: Three dots are one character, …, so they cannot break across lines.

Alternative: Butterick agrees and also asks for a no-break space on a side with no text so the ellipsis stays with its text; the engine does not do that (`... and so` keeps an ordinary space, a gap in the backlog). UAX #14 gives … class IN, so it is never split from another ellipsis. Interrupted speech: Butterick prefers an em dash outside fiction; the engine leaves it.

Sources: [butterick-ellipsis](#src-butterick-ellipsis), [uax14](#src-uax14)

### `space.before-punctuation`

Confidence: `general-knowledge`.

Why: A space before , . ; : ! ? ) is a typing slip in English and most languages; French keeps it before : ; ! ?, so the rule leaves those alone there.

Alternative: Opt-in rule (off unless named in `enable`). No source was read for it; the choice rests on general knowledge. typograf and typopo do the same.

### `space.after-comma`

Confidence: `general-knowledge`.

Why: A comma is followed by a space; a,b is a typing slip.

Alternative: Opt-in rule (off unless named in `enable`). No source was read for it; the choice rests on general knowledge. It can touch non-prose such as decimals written with a comma (1,5) and lists in code, which is why it is opt-in.

### `punctuation.double`

Confidence: `general-knowledge`.

Why: A run of ! or ? is shortened to one mark; ?! and !? are kept because they are deliberate.

Alternative: Opt-in rule (off unless named in `enable`). No source was read for it; the choice rests on general knowledge. Butterick treats repeated exclamation marks as a style question, not a typesetting one (see the backlog).

### `dash.dialogue`

Confidence: `verified`.

Why: In these languages a line of dialogue opens with a dash, not a quotation mark, so a paragraph that opens with a typed hyphen and a space is such a line. The dash is the one the language uses for it, followed by a no-break space (attached with no space in Spanish), in both registers.

Alternative: Which dash: the sources set the em dash for uk, es and pt; pl uses whatever dash the text uses (the pack uses the em dash); fr accepts both (em set); bg says long dash but renders the en dash and it shows the en dash, and for both the pack keeps the em dash of its ISG sentence dash so that dialogue and the narrator match; ro, hr, hu, sk, lt, cs, sl, fi and sv set the en dash; da says the same form as the tankestreg, the em dash in the pack. Only the OQLF asks for a no-break space after the dash; the engine sets one everywhere but Spanish so that the dash never ends a line alone. Not set: et (quotes are the norm, EKI), lv and el (the dash is not confirmed by a source read), and en, de, ch, nl, mt, ga (dialogue is quoted). A paragraph whose later line also opens with a dash is read as a list and left alone. The 2019 Ukrainian Pravopys (§ 161) could not be read; § 125 of the earlier edition was. In cs, sl and da a dash is one of several allowed forms.

### `number.group`

Confidence: `general-knowledge`.

Why: Integers of five or more digits are grouped in threes with the language separator (comma, no-break space, dot) so a long number can be read.

Alternative: Opt-in rule (off unless named in `enable`). The SI brochure (9th ed., 5.4.4) says digits "may be divided into groups of three by a space", that "neither dots nor commas are ever inserted in the spaces between groups", that with only four digits before or after the decimal marker a space is not used to isolate a single digit, and that the practice "is a matter of choice". The engine agrees on groups of three, on starting at five digits, and on the space itself for the languages whose pack uses a no-break space (the brochure does not say nonbreaking). It deliberately differs where the pack groups with a comma (en) or a dot, which the brochure forbids; that is local custom, not SI. The brochure is a metrology standard and does not speak for ordinary prose in any one language, so the choice stays general-knowledge. Separators per language come from the packs and are otherwise unverified; Swiss usage differs (the pack follows the Federal Chancellery, not read here).

Sources: [bipm-si-brochure-9](#src-bipm-si-brochure-9)

### `dash.sentence`

Confidence: `verified`.

Why: A break in a sentence takes a dash, and its form depends on the language. For English the em dash is backed by the source; it is the engine default.

Alternative: The source sets the em dash flush against the words and calls spaces acceptable; the engine sets it spaced, with a no-break space before it, which matches neither. A search result quoting Bringhurst (The Elements of Typographic Style, 2nd ed., section 5.2.1, not read here) says he prefers a spaced en dash in English, so the primary guides disagree among themselves. UAX #14 gives the em dash class B2, which allows a break on both sides, hence the no-break space before it. The spaced en dash for German and for British literary English is not backed by any source read yet. For ro, hr, hu, sk and sv the spaced en dash comes from the dialogue sources: each sets the same spaced en dash before the words of the narrator, and the packs had no verified sentence dash before.

Sources: [butterick-dashes](#src-butterick-dashes), [uax14](#src-uax14)

Why: +- before a digit is the plus-minus sign ±.

### `symbol.minus`

Confidence: `general-knowledge`.

Why: A minus sign is −, not a hyphen.

Alternative: The SI brochure, 9th ed., has no passage on the minus sign: its section 5.4.4 only shows the leading zero (-0.234 but not -.234) and says nothing of the character to use, so it does not back this rule. Unicode only shows that the character exists; it does not say when to use it. Butterick says to use an en dash for the minus sign (the "Math symbols" page also lists the &minus; entity) and calls an en dash acceptable in spreadsheets, so a style guide that prefers − over – was not found. Still unchecked.

Sources: [unicode-names](#src-unicode-names), [butterick-dashes](#src-butterick-dashes), [butterick-math](#src-butterick-math)

Confidence: `verified`.

### `space.percent`

Confidence: `verified`.

Why: German and French set a no-break space before % and a currency sign. CLDR agrees for % in de and fr, and for the currency sign in de and fr; it sets no space before % in en, uk, pl, de-CH and fr-CH, which is what the engine does. OQLF, a printed-style authority, agrees for French: a no-break space before %, $ and €. For € alone, the Greek, Finnish, Italian and Danish editions of the Interinstitutional Style Guide (part 3, 7) write the symbol after the figure with a space (30 €), as does the Czech handbook (250 €, with a fixed space), so currencySpace stays on in el, fi, it, da and cs.

Alternative: The space before % and the space before € are two pack fields, percentSpace and currencySpace: Irish, Maltese, Portuguese, Latvian, Lithuanian and Estonian set the first and not the second, because their ISG editions write €30 with no space or show money only as the code EUR (see each pack's notes). CLDR also puts a no-break space before the currency sign in uk, pl and fr-CH; the engine leaves 49€ alone there (a gap, in the backlog). OQLF also asks for a no-break space before a French colon, and none or a fine space before ; ! and ?, which the engine does not set (a gap, in the backlog). OQLF is a Quebec source; a France or Swiss guide may differ (a fine space). The German side rests on CLDR only; no Duden page was read. The ISG passage is for the euro only (other currency signs are never spaced by the engine) and, apart from the Finnish edition, does not say the space is nonbreaking; the Czech handbook was read as a fetch-tool summary. The ISG percent rule (6.4) cited in the packs was not re-read here. Of the country guides named in the packs (Kielitoimiston ohjepankki, Retskrivningsordbogen, Treccani), none was read for €.

Sources: [cldr-numbers](#src-cldr-numbers), [oqlf-espacement](#src-oqlf-espacement), [isg-2022-euro](#src-isg-2022-euro), [ijp-penezni-castky](#src-ijp-penezni-castky), [uax14](#src-uax14)

- `de`: [cldr-numbers](#src-cldr-numbers)
- `fr`: [cldr-numbers](#src-cldr-numbers), [oqlf-espacement](#src-oqlf-espacement)

### `symbol.degree`

Confidence: `verified`.

Why: A temperature or angle unit gets a no-break space before it (20 °C), and the degree sign is added where the unit letter stands alone.

Alternative: Opt-in rule (off unless named in `enable`). The SI brochure, 9th ed., 5.4.3: a space always separates the unit from the number, so "t = 30.2 °C but not t = 30.2°C"; the one exception is the plane-angle degree, minute and second, which take no space (30° 22′ 8″). The engine agrees: it puts a space before ° only when a C or F follows, and leaves a bare angle degree (30°) alone. The brochure does not say the space is nonbreaking (our choice), and it covers the Celsius scale only: the Fahrenheit case (°F) is not SI, so no source was read for it. The label guard (Type 2 F is not a temperature) and the English-only rule for a bare C or F are the engine's own.

Sources: [bipm-si-brochure-9](#src-bipm-si-brochure-9)

- `en`: [bipm-si-brochure-9](#src-bipm-si-brochure-9)

### `space.section`

Confidence: `verified`.

Why: A § or ¶ stays on the line of the number it cites. Butterick: a mark "should always be followed by a nonbreaking space"; the page shows the break between § and the number as the error. The nonbreaking-spaces page repeats it for § and ¶.

Alternative: The source says "always" and so would also have a space inserted in §5; the engine only turns a typed space into a no-break space, in the same way as ©2026 and €30 are left as typed. The page says to double the mark for several references and to spell the word out at a sentence start; the engine does neither, it only keeps the mark with its number. English only: the source does not cover how other languages set § (German and French practice, for one, is not checked). Read as a fetch-tool summary with quotes; not compared with other tools.

Sources: [butterick-section-marks](#src-butterick-section-marks), [butterick-nbsp](#src-butterick-nbsp)

- `en`: [butterick-section-marks](#src-butterick-section-marks)
